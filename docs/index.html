Scheme manual

Everything a scheme can use, what it costs, and what it cannot do. Read this instead of the source code. The source remains the final authority: the interface text is TEXT in hbf_eval/contract.py.

Repository: github.com/envymoon/HBF_ANN_eval · how to run: README

1. Overview

A scheme is one Python file with three entry functions. The evaluator calls them in order:

build(vectors, ctx) → indexOffline, once per dataset. Sees all base vectors. Builds any data structure.
not timed
layout(index, vectors, ctx) → planOffline. Sees the device's datasheet. Decides which bytes go to HBF pages, HBM and per-channel SRAM.
not timed
serve(state, events, ctx) → stateOnline. Called at every wake. Issues reads, computes, answers queries.
timed
  • The one rule to design around: serve never sees vectors or index. It sees only what layout placed, and every access to it goes through ctx, which bills time.
  • Time in serve is simulated, not measured. HBF reads run on an event-level device model; GPU work is billed from tensor shapes; HBM and SRAM reads are billed by bandwidth and latency. The same inputs always give the same result.
  • The host sweeps ctx.effort from 0 to 1 to draw a recall–throughput curve. The score reads that curve at recall@10 = 0.90 (also 0.95, 0.99).

2. Can I express this?

Look up the kind of design you have in mind. Yes works directly; Partly works with a workaround or a restriction; No cannot be expressed in the current version.

Index and data structures

DesignHow, or why not
Any index: graph, IVF, PQ / SQ / OPQ, LSH, trees, multi-level, hybridsYes build is arbitrary torch code over the base vectors (1 h wall-clock budget at 100K, 2 h at 1M). The index may be at most 16× the data size.
Learned components (routers, learned quantizers, small networks)Partly Train in build with plain tensor code and torch.autograd.grad. torch.nn and torch.optim are not available: write the layers and the update step yourself. Inference in serve is billed like any tensor op.
Using the query distribution while buildingNo build and layout see only base vectors. Queries arrive only in serve. You can learn from them online (see "online adaptation" below).
Custom compression or bit packing (4-bit codes, delta coding, mixed precision)Yes HBF pages are raw bytes (uint8[P, 4096]), so pack anything. Decoding in serve is billed as tensor ops.
Replicating data (hot records on several pages or channels)Yes HBF capacity is 3× the data size per HBF position, so with 4 HBF positions you have about 12× the data size.
Changing the index during serving (insert, delete, rebuild)No HBF pages are read-only in serve; HBF writes are not simulated. Dynamic updates are planned work.

Placement

DesignHow, or why not
Choose which bytes share a page (co-locate neighbours, page-aligned records)Yes You write plan["pages"] byte by byte.
Choose which channel, die and bank a page lives onYes Placement is a fixed formula of the page number and plan["interleave"] (see where a page lives). Order your pages to put them where you want; ctx.mapping confirms it in serve.
Static tables in HBM (PQ codes, centroids, entry points, adjacency of hot nodes)Yes plan["hbm"], up to spec["hbm_bytes"] (5%–25% of the data size, drawn per seed).
Per-channel scratchpad SRAM on the HBF base diePartly plan["sram"]; row r lives on channel r mod channels. The size per channel is drawn per seed from 0 to 2 MiB and can be 0: a scheme that needs SRAM must work without it.
Choosing the hardware (how many HBM vs HBF positions, tR, channel count)No The platform and the device are fixed by the task. A scheme can read them (ctx.spec()) and adapt, but not change them. To see how a scheme depends on HBM, run it on --data clustered_m1 etc.

Serving and scheduling

DesignHow, or why not
Asynchronous reads, pipelining, speculation, prefetchYes ctx.submit returns at once; completions arrive in later events. Keep as many reads in flight as you like (up to 221).
Partial-page reads (read 64 B instead of 4 KiB)Yes 64 B to 4 KiB, 64 B aligned, inside one page. A partial read still senses the whole page; it saves only link time.
Batching and coalescing across queriesYes One serve call handles every open query; dedupe, sort and merge their reads however you like.
Scheduling by physical location (per-unit ordering, avoiding bank conflicts)Yes ctx.mapping(pages) gives channel, die and bank. The order you submit is the order requests reach each unit.
Reusing a page that is still in a page bufferPartly Each bank has 2 page buffers and a re-read of a buffered page is a hit (no sense). The replacement policy (LRU or FIFO) is hidden and drawn per seed, so design for "soon after" rather than for one policy.
Batch Read (dispatch now instead of waiting for the base-die timer)Yes ctx.submit(..., batch=True).
Per-query control flow (priority queues, per-query beam width, early exit)Yes Inside for i in ctx.lanes(n): branches stay on the GPU. Outside a lane, every Python-value read-back is a sync.
Timed wake-ups (wait for k completions or a deadline)Yes ctx.wait(count, timeout_us).
Online adaptation (caches filled while serving, learned prefetch tables, statistics)Yes Reserve space in layout, fill it with ctx.hbm_write / ctx.sram_write, or keep small things in state. Sizes are fixed by layout. Every effort level starts again from layout's contents.
Refresh scheduling (when to pay read-disturb refresh)Yes plan["refresh"] = "scheme", then ctx.debt() and ctx.refresh().
Power-aware schedulingPartly The budget and energy per sense are in ctx.spec(), so you can pace senses yourself. There is no call that reports the budget's current level.
Observing device queues or unit occupancyPartly Only your own in-flight reads (ctx.pending()) and the clock (ctx.now()). Unit queues are not visible, as on real hardware.

Compute and platform

DesignHow, or why not
Any tensor arithmetic on the GPU (distances, GEMM, top-k, sort, scatter, bit ops)Yes Every torch op is billed from its shapes (section 7).
Lower precision for speed (fp16, bf16, int8)Partly Smaller dtypes move fewer bytes, so memory-bound ops get cheaper. Flops are counted the same for every dtype: there is no tensor-core speed-up.
Custom CUDA kernels, operator fusion, warp-level tricksNo Only torch ops are billed. A wake already runs as one fused kernel (no launch cost per op), and ctx.lanes models per-block code; nothing finer is modelled.
Host CPU computation, host DRAM, an SSD tierNo The platform is GPU + HBM + HBF. Reading a value back to the host costs a sync and host-link bytes; there is no host-side storage.
Multiple GPUs or nodesNoOne GPU per platform.
Filtered search, range search, a different kNo Every task is top-k with k = 10 under the dataset's metric.
External libraries (faiss, numpy, scipy)No Only torch, F (torch.nn.functional) and math, without imports (section 9).
RandomnessYesOnly through ctx.uniform(*shape), which is seeded.
If your design is not in these tables: it is expressible if it can be written as (1) a data structure computed from the base vectors, (2) a placement of bytes into HBF pages, HBM tensors and SRAM tensors, and (3) a serve state machine that uses only the ctx methods and torch ops.

3. The scheme file

"""One sentence describing the idea."""

KNOBS = {                         # name: (default, low, high, scale)
    "nlist":    (1024, 8, 16384, "int"),
    "probe_lo": (4, 1, 256, "int"),
    "probe_hi": (48, 1, 1024, "int"),
}

def build(vectors, ctx): ...       # exactly 2 positional arguments
def layout(index, vectors, ctx): ...   # exactly 3
def serve(state, events, ctx): ...     # exactly 3
def helper(x, y): ...              # any other top-level function, called by name
  • Allowed at module level: a docstring, one KNOBS literal, top-level defs. Nothing else.
  • Knobs: (default, low, high, scale) with scale "int", "linear" or "log" (needs low > 0). Read a knob only as ctx.knobs["name"] with a literal name; reading an undeclared knob is an error. --set name=value overrides one (clamped to its range).
  • The *_hi convention: --wide sets every knob whose name ends in _hi to its declared maximum. Name the top of your effort range ..._hi so this works.
  • Caching: build and layout results are cached by the code of the functions each one reaches and the knobs those read. Changing serve, or a knob only serve reads, never rebuilds the index. Docstrings and comments do not affect the cache key.
  • Check a file without running it: python3 -m hbf_eval.bench check my/scheme.py (lists knobs and which stage reads each).

The plan

KeyTypeMeaning and limits
"pages"uint8[P, 4096], P ≥ 1 The HBF image: page p holds exactly these bytes. P ≤ (8 − m) × hbf_pages_per_position.
"hbm"{str: tensor} Tensors placed in HBM, read in serve with ctx.hbm(name, rows). Total bytes ≤ hbm_bytes. Any dtype and shape; the first dimension is the row.
"sram"{str: tensor} Tensors placed in the base-die SRAM. Row r lives on channel r mod channels. For each channel, the sum over tensors of ⌈rows / channels⌉ × row bytes must be ≤ sram_bytes_per_channel. Use {} for none.
"interleave"int, 1 to 220 How many consecutive pages go to one channel before moving to the next (see below).
"refresh" (optional)"default" or "scheme" "default": the host pays read-disturb refresh whenever a die is idle and owes a page, and at once when a plane owes more than refresh_slack_pages. "scheme": refresh is paid only when serve calls ctx.refresh; the device still forces payment above 2 × refresh_slack_pages.

No other keys are allowed. Writing the pages is free: the image is on the device before the first query, and program time and wear for loading it are not modelled.

Where a page lives

With C HBF channels in total (C = (8 − m) × channels_per_position: 4 at 100K, 8 at 1M), interleave g, B banks per die and Dd dies per channel:

channel = (p // g) % C
local   = g * (p // (g * C)) + p % g        # the page's index within its channel
die     = (local // B) % Dd
bank    = local % B

So with g = 1, consecutive pages go round-robin over channels; with a large g, a run of g pages stays on one channel. Within a channel, consecutive local pages go to consecutive banks. Two pages on the same (channel, die, bank) are sensed one after the other; pages on different banks are sensed in parallel.

6. serve(state, events, ctx) → state

def serve(state, events, ctx): ...; return state

The wake loop

  1. The run starts with N clients (ctx.spec()["clients"]), each with one open query. A client sends its next query the moment its last one is answered (closed loop), until the task's query total is reached.
  2. At each wake the host calls serve(state, events, ctx). events holds everything that arrived since the last wake: new queries and completed HBF reads.
  3. Inside serve, every ctx call and torch op moves the simulated clock forward. HBF reads you submit run on the device meanwhile.
  4. After serve returns, the host sleeps until the wake condition holds: by default one event (a completion or a new query); ctx.wait(count, timeout_us) changes it for the next wake only. If nothing is in flight the host wakes at once.
  5. The run ends when every query is answered. If no read is in flight and serve answers nothing for 1000 wakes in a row, the run fails as a deadlock.

So serve is a state machine: a read issued now completes in a later call. Use the tag you attach to each read to find out, when its data comes back, which query and which item it belonged to.

events

KeyTypeMeaning
"qids"int64[a]ids of the queries that arrived since the last wake
"queries"float32[a, D]their vectors

"nbytes"int64[c]the size of each completed read

state

  • Your dict, returned by one call and passed to the next ({} at the first call).
  • May hold tensors, numbers, strings of at most 64 characters, lists, tuples and dicts with string keys, nested at most 8 deep.
  • Size cap ctx.spec()["state_bytes"] = min(clients × 64 KiB, the GPU's on-chip budget: 16 MiB at 100K, 32 MiB at 1M). It models working state on chip, so it cannot hold the data set.

ctx methods in serve

All tensor arguments are int64 unless stated. Methods marked "lane: deferred" can be called inside a ctx.lanes loop and take effect at that lane's time when the loop ends.

ctx.submit(pages, offsets, nbytes, tags, batch=False) → None
Arguments
pages, offsets, tags: int64[r]; nbytes: int64[r] or one int for all. tags are any integers you choose (for example qid * n + node); they come back in events["tags"].
Rules
Each read: 64 B to 4096 B, offset and size multiples of 64, inside one page; page in [0, P). At most 220 reads per call and 221 in flight.
Effect
Issues the reads now and returns at once. Reads reach each unit in the order given. batch=True is the Batch Read hint: these reads, and any held at the base die, dispatch at once instead of waiting for the base-die timer.
Cost
One kernel entry on the host side (0 µs in the default table). Device time is what the reads take on the device.
Lane
deferred.
ctx.hbm(name, rows=None) → tensor

Arguments
name: a tensor layout placed in that tier. rows: int64 row ids of any shape, or None for the whole tensor.
Returns
tensor[rows] (a copy).

Waits for the data. HBM: bytes / HBM bandwidth + 0.1 µs, with each row rounded up to 32 B. SRAM: the busiest channel's bytes / channel link bandwidth + 0.05 µs, rows rounded up to 64 B; SRAM traffic shares the channel link with HBF transfers (with priority over queued ones).
Lane
allowed; the wait counts toward that lane's latency, and the phase lasts at least until all lanes' traffic is through.
ctx.hbm_write(name, rows, values) → None
ctx.sram_write(name, rows, values) → None
Arguments
rows: distinct int64[r], or None for the whole tensor. values: the tensor's dtype, shape [r, *row shape] (or the full shape).
Effect
Overwrites those rows. The tensor keeps the size and shape layout gave it. Writes last for the rest of the run; the next effort level starts from layout's contents again.
Cost
The same as reading the same rows.
for i in ctx.lanes(n):
Arguments
n: int, 0 to 65 536 (for example one lane per open query).
Effect
Runs the loop body once per lane. Each lane models one thread block on one SM: inside it, reading a tensor value (.item(), bool(), int(), .tolist()) is a branch on the GPU (0.02 µs) instead of a host sync (0.5 µs plus host-link bytes).
Cost
The phase lasts 0.6 µs + max(sum of all lanes' compute, max over lanes of (24 × its compute + its branches + its HBM/SRAM waits)). The lanes together never beat the whole GPU; one lane never beats one SM (1/24 of the GPU).
Rules
The loop must run to its end (no break or return inside it). No nesting. ctx.debt and ctx.refresh are not allowed inside. Use it when control flow differs per query; heavy arithmetic is still cheaper as batched code outside.
ctx.finish(qids, ids) → None
Arguments
qids: int64[a], each an open query, each once. ids: int64[a, k], base-vector ids in [0, n).
Effect
Answers those queries now. Each answered client sends its next query at once; it appears in the next events.
Lane
deferred.
ctx.wait(count=1, timeout_us=inf) → None
Sets the next wake: when count events (completions plus new queries) are waiting, or after timeout_us, or when nothing is in flight, whichever comes first. Applies to the next wake only. Use it to batch more completions per wake (fewer wake costs) at the price of latency.
ctx.mapping(pages) → int64[r, 3]
Channel, die and bank of each page (what a host reads from the configuration registers). Free.
ctx.pending() → int64[...]
Tags of your reads issued and not yet seen complete, in issue order. Free.
ctx.now() → float
Simulated time in µs (inside a lane: that lane's time).
ctx.debt() → float64[dies]
Pages of refresh work each die owes (the most any of its planes owes). Die id = channel × dies_per_channel + die. Not allowed inside a lane.
ctx.refresh(dies, counts=1) → None
Starts counts[i] refresh operations on die dies[i]. One operation waits for the die's reads in progress, draws sense + program energy, then holds the die (or, where programs hold one plane, the plane that owes most) for tR + program time, and retires up to one page of debt per plane. Useful only with plan["refresh"] = "scheme". Not allowed inside a lane.
ctx.spec(), ctx.knobs, ctx.effort, ctx.uniform(*shape)
ctx.spec(): all of layout's fields, plus channels (C), pages (P), interleave, refresh, clients (N), state_bytes (the state cap), hbm and sram ({name: shape} of what layout placed).

ctx.uniform(*shape): seeded uniform samples, billed like writing that many floats.

7. How time is billed

The default billing table models an RTX 4060-class GPU running each wake as one fused kernel.

How flops and bytes are counted

  • Matrix products (matmul, mm, bmm, @, linear, einsum, …): 2 × output elements × inner dimension.
  • cdist: 3 × output elements × dimension.
  • Sorting (sort, argsort, topk, unique, kthvalue, median): 4 × elements × log2(last dimension).
  • Everything else (elementwise, reductions, comparisons): the largest element count among inputs and outputs.
  • Bytes moved: inputs plus outputs. Gathers (x[idx], gather, index_select) count 2 × output + index bytes; scatters and in-place writes count 2 × the written values.
  • Bytes depend on dtype, flops do not: fp16 halves the bytes of a memory-bound op but not the time of a compute-bound one.

What this means for design

  • One op over all open queries costs about the same as one op for one query. Batch across queries.
  • A Python loop over queries outside a lane pays a sync for every value it reads back. Use tensor ops, or a lane.
  • Waiting is not billed as compute, but the clock moves: a read's latency shows up as query latency.
  • Offline work (build, layout) is free, so precompute everything that does not depend on the query.

8. What the device does with a read

The device model follows the OCP HBF specification v0.7.0 and the HBF literature. One read goes through these steps:

  1. Arrive at its channel's base die. Beyond the in-flight cap (mocs) it waits on the host side.
  2. Base-die timer: a normal read is held until the timer fires (0–2 µs, hidden). A Batch Read dispatches the held reads and itself at once.
  3. Dispatch to its unit (die × bank). If the page is already in one of the unit's 2 page buffers, it is a hit: skip to step 6.
  4. Sense queue: the unit senses in strict arrival order. At the head, a read whose page an earlier sense just loaded is also a hit. Otherwise the sense takes a buffer, draws its energy from the stack's power budget (waiting in order when the budget is short; the budget can burst up to 10 tR worth of power), and takes tR (with jitter).
  5. Core-die transfer: the whole 4 KiB page crosses the die's core-die channel into the base die; the unit senses nothing else until it is across.
  6. Link transfer: the requested bytes cross the channel link, one transfer at a time in ready order.
  7. Complete: the read appears in events at the next wake.
  • Read disturb and refresh: every sense adds refresh debt to its unit. Debt is paid by refresh operations (default host policy or your own, see plan["refresh"]). A sense on a plane owing more than refresh_slack_pages may need a retry (one more sense).
  • HBM has its own links, so HBM and HBF transfers run at the same time.
  • What a scheme controls: which pages it reads and when, the order and grouping of submissions, Batch Read, partial reads, where pages live (layout), and when refresh is paid. Everything else is the device.

  • A scheme that never reaches 0.90 scores between −16 and −8 depending on how close it gets.
  • The scheme's score is the mean over the four tasks. Scores at 0.95 and 0.99 are reported too.
  • Reported but not scored: build and layout time, index / HBF / HBM / SRAM bytes, and diagnostics (reads and senses per query, page-buffer hit rate, unit utilisation, compute share, power-wait share, read rounds per query).
